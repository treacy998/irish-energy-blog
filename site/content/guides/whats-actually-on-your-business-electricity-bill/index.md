{{< lead >}}
A commercial electricity bill looks like one number. It isn't. This guide breaks that number into the costs behind it — and separates the charges you can change from the ones you can only manage.
{{< /lead >}}

{{< callout title="The big idea" >}}
The headline unit rate tells you the price on the page. The structure tells you what you are actually paying for.
{{< /callout >}}

## 1. 30c/kWh isn't the whole story

Say your business spends €10,000 a year on electricity. It is worth asking where that €10,000 actually goes — because surprisingly little of the bill is simply the supplier's own price.

A commercial unit rate looks like a single number. It isn't. It bundles together the wholesale cost of the electricity, transmission and distribution charges, market and system costs, the PSO levy, tax and VAT — alongside the supplier's own commercial pricing.

The Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU), in its 29 July 2026 decision approving the 2026/27 network charges, puts the scale of the network component plainly: network costs are "circa 30% of an end user's overall cost of energy."

And that is just the network slice. Dr Paul Deane of University College Cork, in his 2026 explanation of the Irish electricity bill, shows how much of the rest is shaped by international fuel prices, the CRU and the grid operators rather than by the company whose logo appears on the bill.

So the useful question is not simply "who has the lowest headline rate?" It is: what part of this price is actually mine to influence — and what can I do about the rest?

## 2. Three kinds of cost — and three questions

The costs on your bill sort into three broad kinds. The fastest way to tell them apart is to ask the same three questions of any line:

- Who sets it?
- What makes it move?
- Can I do anything about it?

Run any charge through those questions and it lands in one of three buckets:

**1. Competitive — the energy itself.**
Your supplier buys electricity in the wholesale market and prices that exposure into your contract. Wholesale conditions, hedging and the risk of fixing a rate all matter. This is where switching, timing and contract structure genuinely move the number.

**2. Regulated — networks, market and system charges, PSO and tax.**
The underlying rates are determined through the regulatory and statutory framework rather than by whichever supplier happens to bill you. Switching supplier does not make an ESB Networks charge disappear; the supplier generally collects the relevant charge and passes it through.

**3. Connection-dependent — your capacity, or MIC.**
The tariff itself is regulated, but what you pay depends on the characteristics of your own connection. That makes MIC the unusual case: you cannot negotiate the published capacity rate, but you can sometimes reduce the amount of capacity you are paying for.

{{< callout type="note" title="A useful distinction" >}}
Some costs can be negotiated. Others can only be managed.
{{< /callout >}}

## 3. From power station to your door

Buying electricity is only half the problem. You also have to get it from where it is generated to your premises — and that journey has a cost of its own.

Think of the physical journey as:

{{< callout type="note" title="Generation → Transmission → Distribution → Your business" >}}
The power moves through the national high-voltage network and then through the local distribution network to your connection.
{{< /callout >}}

Two of those stages appear on the bill as network charges:

- **TUoS — Transmission Use of System:** the charge associated with the national, high-voltage transmission network operated by EirGrid.
- **DUoS — Distribution Use of System:** the charge associated with the distribution network operated by ESB Networks.

The important point is that these charges are regulated pass-throughs. The CRU approves the relevant revenues and tariffs; the network companies publish the charging arrangements; suppliers recover the charges from customers.

A typical business connected to the distribution system pays both TUoS and DUoS. A customer connected directly to the transmission system pays TUoS.

There is another important layer: your connection itself. The DUoS rules assign connections to tariff groups according to their characteristics. For business customers, the distinction between DG5 and DG6 is especially important, with 50 kVA marking the main threshold for the low-voltage maximum-demand tariff.

That means the network cost is not simply "another few cents per kWh". It depends on the kind of connection you have, the tariff group it sits in and — for DG6 — the capacity you have contracted. The charging year then resets on 1 October, so a fixed supplier energy price does not necessarily mean a fixed all-in bill.

In one line: EirGrid → transmission; ESB Networks → distribution; CRU → regulatory approval.

## 4. Energy — the electricity itself

Here's something that catches a lot of business owners out: your building did not change, your machines did not change, your annual consumption did not change — and your electricity rate still went up. How?

Because electricity is unusual. It cannot be stored at scale in the same way as most commodities, so generation and consumption have to be kept in balance in real time. The wholesale market therefore clears at the marginal cost of the last unit of electricity needed to meet demand in each hour.

Dr Paul Deane of UCC describes it as the market-cleared price equal to the marginal cost of the last unit required to meet demand. In Ireland, that marginal generator is very often gas.

> "strongly correlated to European and British natural gas prices"
>
> — Dr Paul Deane, University College Cork

That matters because gas does not have to supply every unit of electricity to influence the price of the market. When a gas plant is the marginal generator, movements in European gas prices can feed through into Irish wholesale electricity prices even though nothing has changed at the individual business.

Deane's 2026 figures show the scale of the movement: the wholesale price averaged about €144/MWh in May 2026, while the preceding year moved through a much lower and wider range. The underlying commodity price does not sit still — and suppliers have to manage that exposure.

Suppliers do not simply buy the spot price and hand it straight to you. They hedge by buying electricity ahead of time, smoothing some of the volatility. Hedging can delay the effect of a price shock, but it does not remove the underlying market exposure.

Nor does more wind automatically mean lower bills all the time. Wind generation can push the wholesale price down by displacing more expensive generation, but the system remains exposed to gas on periods of low wind, to interconnection with Britain, to network constraints, balancing requirements and demand.

{{< callout title="The practical point" >}}
The energy component is the part of your bill that behaves most like a market — and therefore the part a supplier or broker can actually do something about through when and how you buy.
{{< /callout >}}

## 5. The networks — paying to get the electricity here

The national electricity grid is the infrastructure between the generator and your business. The cost of running, maintaining and expanding that infrastructure is recovered through TUoS and DUoS.

For the local network, DUoS covers the lower-voltage system that ultimately delivers electricity to your premises. ESB Networks' network includes poles, cables, substations and transformers. For the national backbone, TUoS recovers the cost of the high-voltage transmission system operated by EirGrid.

These charges are not optional extras invented by your supplier. They are regulated charges, recovered from suppliers and generally passed through to customers.

**DUoS is not the same for every business.**
Connections sit in different DUoS tariff groups, from domestic categories through DG5 and DG6 for low-voltage businesses and into higher-voltage categories for larger industrial customers. Most SMEs will encounter DG5 or DG6.

The DG5/DG6 boundary is particularly important: the low-voltage maximum-demand tariff, DG6, generally applies where MIC is 50 kVA or more, while smaller business connections sit in DG5, subject to the relevant rules and legacy arrangements.

**The group stays; the rates move.**
Your tariff group is linked to the connection, but the euro rates attached to it are reset each charging year. So a business can sign a fixed supplier energy contract and still see the pass-through portion of its bill change when regulated rates are reset.

**TUoS is the national motorway.**
If DUoS is the local road network, TUoS is the motorway network moving power around the country in bulk. A distribution-connected business generally pays both: TUoS for transmission and DUoS for distribution.

The July 2026 CRU decision also points to the scale of the wider network programme, with up to €18 billion of expenditure required over the next five years. The immediate point for a business is simple: network charges are a regulated cost, but they are not a frozen cost.

## 6. The capacity charge — you pay for the pipe, not the water

Here's the one that catches people out: you can pay thousands of euro a year for electrical capacity you never actually use.

Think of your connection as a pipe into the building. Your annual consumption, measured in kWh, is the water flowing through it. But you are also charged for the size of the pipe: the maximum load the network has agreed to make available to your premises.

That is your Maximum Import Capacity, or MIC, measured in kVA.

{{< callout type="note" title="Keep these two numbers separate" >}}
**kWh** = how much electricity you used. **kVA of MIC** = how much capacity the network must be ready to supply.
{{< /callout >}}

On the 2026/27 DG6 tariff, the capacity charge is €57.37 per kVA per year. A business with 100 kVA of MIC therefore carries €5,737 a year in capacity charges before it uses a single unit of electricity, on top of the DG6 standing charge of €1,537.22.

Why charge for capacity at all? Because capacity costs money to provide. The network must be able to support the maximum demand it has agreed to serve, with sufficient infrastructure and capacity to do so.

That is why two businesses can use exactly the same amount of electricity over a year and still face very different capacity costs. A 100 kVA connection at €57.37/kVA costs €5,737 a year. A 500 kVA connection costs €28,685 a year — regardless of whether the customer regularly uses all of that capacity.

Businesses can also end up carrying more MIC than they need. Demand may have been higher in the past; machinery may have been removed; the connection may have been deliberately oversized; a short, simultaneous peak may have shaped the original requirement; future expansion may have been planned; or several meters or connections may be involved.

The right question is not "is my MIC high?" It is "does my MIC still match my real maximum demand, with sensible headroom for what is actually planned?"

Right-sizing MIC is one of the few network-linked savings a business can actually act on, and it has nothing to do with switching supplier.

{{< callout type="warning" title="⚠️ Don't cut MIC blindly" >}}
ESB Networks' Statement of Charges provides for a surcharge of "6 × capacity charge rate × excess kVA" where standard (NQH) installations exceed MIC, with a 5× provision for interval (QH) installations, on the relevant excess. Right-size to real demand — don't guess.
{{< /callout >}}

## 7. The costs you don't get to choose

Four more things can sit on the bill without being set by your supplier: market and system charges, the PSO levy, Electricity Tax and VAT. They do different jobs, and one of them has a particularly unusual relationship with electricity prices.

### Market and system charges: the cost of running a real electricity system

You may see these described as market charges, SEM charges, system charges or Imperfections. They cover costs that arise because the real power system is not frictionless.

> "the cost difference between operating an ideal power system and operating a real power system"
>
> — Dr Paul Deane, University College Cork

In an ideal system, generation and demand would match perfectly, renewable output would be fully predictable and the network would impose no constraints. The real system has reserves, transmission constraints, losses, forecast errors and other operational requirements. Those costs have to be paid for somewhere.

Your supplier pays the market and system charges and generally passes them through to customers. There is deliberately no 2026/27 Imperfections €/MWh figure here because the relevant charging decision was not yet available in this draft's source set.

### PSO — the charge that can move against the market

The Public Service Obligation levy supports renewable electricity schemes. For 2026/27, the CRU has set the PSO funding requirement at €41.48 million, a 66% reduction from the previous year.

For 2026/27 the monthly charge is €1.93 for small commercial customers and €0.24 per kVA of MIC for medium and large commercial customers.

The more interesting story is not the number. It is the direction.

The PSO can move in the opposite direction to wholesale electricity prices. Where supported renewable generators are receiving support relative to market revenues, the required levy can rise when market prices are low and fall when market prices are high. That produces a striking recent history:

{{< callout type="note" title="PSO history" >}}
2022/23 — rebate → 2023/24 — zero → 2024/25 — positive levy → 2025/26 — levy → 2026/27 — smaller levy
{{< /callout >}}

In the depths of the 2022 price spike, the PSO could actually work in customers' favour. It is a useful reminder that "regulated" does not mean "always rising".

### Electricity Tax — small, but mandatory

Revenue currently levies Electricity Tax at €1 per MWh on business electricity — equivalent to 0.1c/kWh. That business rate has been in force since 1 January 2020. Limited reliefs apply to certain qualifying uses.

### VAT — the final layer

Commercial electricity is currently subject to VAT at 9%, with Budget 2026 providing for that rate to remain until 31 December 2030. For a fully VAT-recoverable business, VAT is generally recoverable through the VAT system; where it cannot be fully recovered, it is a genuine cost.

## 8. Put it all together — one business, one bill

We've spent the first half of this article taking the bill apart. Now let's put one back together.

Take a realistic site with 100 kVA MIC, 200,000 kWh annual consumption, DG6, and assume 70% of consumption occurs in the day and 30% at night.

Using the 2026/27 DG6 rates, the verified components look like this:

| Component | Annual cost, ex-VAT | What determines it? | Can you act? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy + supplier margin | The balance | Market + contract | Yes |
| DUoS — day / night | €6,655 | DG group + consumption | No |
| DUoS — capacity | €5,737 | MIC | Yes — right-size |
| DUoS — standing | €1,537 | Tariff | Potentially |
| PSO | €288 | Regulation | No |
| Electricity Tax | €200 | Tax rate | No |
| TUoS + system services | Not calculated | Regulated | No |
| Imperfections | Not included | 2026/27 rate pending | No |

The DUoS consumption charge, capacity charge, standing charge, PSO and Electricity Tax already total approximately €14,400 a year.

That is before TUoS, system services and Imperfections are added.

At an illustrative 30c/kWh, 200,000 kWh represents about €60,000 before VAT. The verified lines above therefore account for almost a quarter of that amount before several other regulated charges have been included.

That percentage is deliberately not presented as an exact "regulated share". The example excludes the 2026/27 Imperfections charge because it was not yet published in the source set, while a precise TUoS calculation depends on the relevant charging periods and customer characteristics.

{{< callout title="The lesson" >}}
A single headline rate hides a mixture of commodity cost, network infrastructure, a connection-size charge, system costs, policy charges and tax. Only some of those costs are something your supplier is actually competing over.
{{< /callout >}}

## 9. What can you actually control?

You've now seen the bill. Here's the practical question: what can a business actually do about it?

The answer is not simply "switch supplier". Some costs can be changed; others can only be managed.

| Bill component | Can you change the price? | Can you optimise the cost? |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Yes | Yes |
| Supplier charges / standing charge | Yes | Yes |
| DUoS / TUoS | No | Usually no |
| PSO / Electricity Tax | No | No |
| MIC / capacity | No | Yes |
| Consumption timing | No | Yes |
| Efficiency | No | Yes |

You cannot negotiate the network's tariff. It is regulated and applies regardless of which supplier sends the bill.

But that does not mean every network-related cost is fixed from your perspective. You can investigate whether your MIC still matches actual demand. You can look at whether electricity use can be shifted away from expensive periods. You can reduce the amount of electricity you need through efficiency.

And you can shop around for the part of the bill suppliers actually compete over: the energy component and the commercial terms around it.

{{< callout type="note" title="Central lesson" >}}
Some costs can be negotiated. Others can only be managed.
{{< /callout >}}

## 10. The 30.5c vs 31c trap

Which is cheaper: a supplier quoting 30.5c/kWh or one quoting 31.0c/kWh?

On 200,000 kWh a year, the 0.5c difference is €1,000. That's real money.

But you still cannot tell which offer is cheaper from those two numbers alone.

Before comparing two quotes, ask:

- What's actually included in the rate — energy only, or energy plus some pass-through charges?
- What's the standing charge?
- How are DUoS, TUoS, PSO and other pass-throughs treated — bundled, separately itemised or passed through as they change?
- What's the contract — how long is it, and is the rate fixed or variable?
- What happens at renewal, and what does the customer's actual consumption profile look like?

A lower headline rate is therefore not automatically a lower total cost.

{{< callout type="note" title="Compare like with like" >}}
Normalise first. Then compare.
{{< /callout >}}

## 11. Why is the network getting more expensive?

We've established that network charges are regulated and passed through to customers. So why are they getting more expensive?

Because Ireland isn't simply maintaining the electricity network it already has. It is rebuilding and expanding it for a much more electrified economy.

In December 2025, the CRU published its final determination for Price Review 6 (PR6), the five-year regulatory framework covering 2026–2030. The programme paves the way for up to €18.9 billion of investment in Ireland's energy infrastructure, supported by €3.5 billion of Government equity investment.

> "the most significant period of energy infrastructure development since rural electrification"
>
> — Minister Darragh O'Brien, on publication of PR6

The programme includes provision for:

- connecting up to 300,000 new homes by 2030
- network infrastructure for up to 1 million electric vehicles and a major heat-pump rollout
- more than 500 capital projects, including 27 new 110 kV substations and 11 new 38 kV substations
- 50,000 pole replacements and hundreds of kilometres of new lines and cables
- readiness to receive 5 GW of offshore wind in the early 2030s

The July 2026 CRU network-charge decision gives a more immediate sense of the scale of the change. For 2026/27, the CRU approved network revenue allowances of €1.5825 billion for distribution and €1.5024 billion for transmission. These represent increases of 18.4% for distribution and 8.1% for transmission against the relevant 2026 allowed revenues.

The Government argues that the investment should ultimately help integrate more renewable generation, reduce constraint and curtailment costs, strengthen resilience and reduce dependence on volatile imported fossil fuels. In the near term, however, part of the investment is reflected in the network charges businesses see on their bills.

{{< callout type="note" title="The bigger picture" >}}
The network is not simply maintaining the electricity system of ten years ago. It is being rebuilt for an electrified economy.
{{< /callout >}}

### A decade of DG6 charges

| DG6 charge | 2016/17 | 2026/27 | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day | 2.040c/kWh | 4.525c/kWh | +121.8% |
| Night | 0.240c/kWh | 0.534c/kWh | +122.5% |
| Capacity | €25.78/kVA | €57.37/kVA | +122.5% |
| Standing | €692.01 | €1,537.22 | +122.1% |

DG6 distribution charges are therefore roughly 122% higher than a decade ago — more than double the 2016/17 schedule at each of these headline components.

That does not mean every part of an electricity bill has doubled, nor that the increase is the result of one decision. It does show the scale of change in the regulated distribution component, and it sits alongside the largest period of grid investment in generations.

## 12. The five-minute electricity bill check

You don't need to become an electricity-market expert to understand your bill. Take your latest statement and find these five things:

1. **DG code** — DG5? DG6? Something else? This tells you which charging structure applies to the connection.
2. **MIC** — How many kVA? Then ask whether that still reflects how the business actually operates.
3. **Annual consumption** — How many kWh? This is the number most people look at first. It isn't the only one that matters.
4. **Contract end date** — When can you actually act? A brilliant comparison is of limited use if the existing contract still has months to run.
5. **Energy vs. pass-through** — How much are you paying for the electricity itself, and how much is made up of regulated charges?

Bonus: look at your consumption profile. A business with the same annual consumption as another can still have a different cost profile depending on when its electricity is being used.

{{< callout title="The five-minute test" >}}
If you know those five things, you already understand more about your electricity contract than the headline c/kWh tells you.
{{< /callout >}}

## 13. Conclusion — read the bill as a stack, not a number

Back to where we started.

Which is cheaper: 30c/kWh or 31c/kWh?

You cannot answer that from the headline rate alone.

That number is the final result of several very different costs: the electricity itself, the transmission and distribution networks, system and market charges, your connection capacity, the PSO, tax and the commercial terms your supplier has offered you.

Some of those costs are regulated. Some depend on your individual connection. Some can be managed. And one major component — the energy price — is genuinely competitive between suppliers.

So the next time a bill lands, don't read it as one number.

{{< callout type="note" title="Read it as a stack" >}}
energy → network → system → policy → tax → contract
{{< /callout >}}

Then run the five-minute check.

The headline rate tells you what the bill looks like. **The structure tells you what you're actually paying for.**
